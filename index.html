Emergent Urban Social Resilience

How do social actors, relations and practices gain agency in producing urban social resilience through the interplay of social, institutional and material-spatial dimensions, and how can mapping, co-design and planning processes strengthen that agency in regenerative green-social infrastructures (GSIs)? Guided by care ethics, participatory and interactive planning, and social innovation, this proposal investigates how urban social resilience can emerge and last when diverse actors gain real agency over local green commons.

Aalborg Living Lab Open Questions: Who maintains local green commons, where decision rights sit, and how everyday care becomes visible to planning. GSIs are understood as circular infrastructures that close loops of biomass, water, tools and knowledge.

Research Gap & Framework: The literature documents projectification and stresses the value of participation, yet says less about how agency across human and non-human actors changes over time, or how informal care arrangements get embedded in statutory planning . I address this with three elements: longitudinal relational actor mapping, co-designed stewardship charters, and a four-pillar agency matrix (decision rungs, deliberative authority, temporal agency and spatial control rights)—forming a Resilient Urban Commons Framework for WeGrowCircle.

Guiding Perspective 1

Care Ethics (Tronto, 1993; Williams, 2020)

Care ethics works through four dispositions: attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness . In urban research it asks who cares for what, who is cared for and who is left out, including more-than-human actors , and makes visible the maintenance and repair that usually go unnoticed .

  • Attentiveness (Caring about): Recognising unattended needs across human and more-than-human worlds .
  • Responsibility (Taking care of): Assuming collective stewardship rather than deferring .
  • Competence (Care-giving): Making visible everyday maintenance and repair .
  • Responsiveness (Care-receiving): Hearing who is cared for and who is left out .
Guiding Perspective 2

Participatory & Interactive Planning

Brings residents, designers, planners and public-sector actors together over time. Here co-production means jointly making maps, prototypes and stewardship charters.

Urban Green Commons : Stewardship is analysed through Ostrom's design principles and research on urban green commons, which defines these commons by the right to manage green space collectively rather than by who owns the land.
Guiding Perspective 3

Social Innovation (Moulaert et al., 2013)

Combines the satisfaction of unmet needs, changed social relations and empowerment . It lets us ask how new relations, practices and rules emerge, how they shift who holds agency, and whether they are institutionalised beyond the project.

Circular Infrastructures: GSIs are understood as circular infrastructures that close loops of biomass, water, tools and knowledge.

Research Gap & Three-Element Response: Resilient Urban Commons Framework

The literature documents projectification and stresses the value of participation. It says less about how agency across human and non-human actors changes over time, or how informal care arrangements get embedded in statutory planning . This proposal addresses this with three elements: longitudinal relational actor mapping, co-designed stewardship charters, and a four-pillar agency matrix (decision rungs, deliberative authority, temporal agency and spatial control rights). Together they form a Resilient Urban Commons Framework that WeGrowCircle can use as a shared conceptual and methodological base.

Operationalising Agency Four-Pillar Agency Matrix [✎]

Four-Pillar Agency Matrix

Part of the Resilient Urban Commons Framework to trace how agency across human and non-human actors changes over time:

1. Decision Rungs :

Consultation → Advisory influence → Shared partnership → Delegated citizen control over green commons.

2. Deliberative Authority :

Who participates, how actors communicate and deliberate, and how informal decisions connect to municipal authority.

3. Temporal Agency :

Everyday care routines (iterational), long-term commons vision (projective), and adapting to emergent pressures (practical-evaluative).

4. Spatial Control Rights :

Collective management rights to alter, plant, maintain, and steward urban green commons regardless of land ownership.

Emergent urban social resilience: mapping agency in actor landscapes and co-designing regenerative green-social infrastructures as urban commons

Preliminary research proposal for the PhD position in Urban Social Resilience within Sustainability and Planning (Ref. 2026/1201), Aalborg University. Applicant: Reni C. Ratriyani

1. Focus and problem

Cities increasingly turn to green-social infrastructures (GSIs), such as shared gardens, courtyards and stormwater landscapes, to respond to climate pressure and social fragmentation. Much of this work is organised as experimentation, and urban living labs have become a common vehicle for it in Europe (Torrens & von Wirth, 2021 ). The projects are largely funded externally and for a limited time. Torrens and von Wirth (2021) call this the projectification of urban change processes and argue that it encourages short-termism and unambitious incrementalism. Bulkeley et al. (2019) , reviewing forty European living labs, find that most have an ambiguous relationship to sustainability transition pathways and that their transformative potential is more modest than the claims made for them.

For the Aalborg Living Lab, three questions remain open: who maintains local green commons, where decision rights sit, and how everyday care becomes visible to planning. Care-focused urban research has mostly looked at interpersonal care and welfare services rather than planning and governance (Power & Williams, 2020 ), and Williams and Rodela (2025) call for more work on how care-based urban strategies last and spread. This proposal asks how urban social resilience can emerge and last when diverse actors gain real agency over local green commons, and how planning practice can support that. It responds to WeGrowCircle and is a starting point to be developed with the supervisors.

2. Research questions

Aim. To understand how urban social resilience emerges from social, institutional and material-spatial dynamics, and to strengthen it by mapping how actors gain agency and co-designing regenerative GSIs as urban commons together with local planning practice.

Main question. How do social actors, relations and practices gain agency in producing urban social resilience through the interplay of social, institutional and material-spatial dimensions, and how can mapping, co-design and planning processes strengthen that agency in regenerative GSIs?
  • SQ1 (mapping). Which actor landscapes, relations, practices and spatial conditions shape resilience around GSIs in the Aalborg Living Lab, and where are the gaps in agency and inclusion?
  • SQ2 (co-design). How can an inclusive co-design process build agency and resilience capacities among diverse actors?
  • SQ3 (stewardship and planning). Which stewardship and governance arrangements sustain resilience, and how can they be embedded in local planning and climate action?

3. Conceptual framework

The project takes a socio-material planning and design perspective. Resilience is treated as an emergent quality produced by interactions among social dynamics, institutional frameworks, and material and spatial configurations. I follow the evolutionary reading of resilience (Davoudi et al., 2012 ), which sees it as the capacity of systems to adapt and transform, so that it becomes a question of agency, institutions and power as well as system behaviour. Relational planning (Healey, 2007 ) provides the planning perspective.

Three perspectives guide the work. Care ethics (Tronto, 1993 ) works through four dispositions: attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness (see Williams, 2020 ). In urban research it asks who cares for what, who is cared for and who is left out, including more-than-human actors (Power & Williams, 2020 ), and it makes visible the maintenance and repair that usually go unnoticed (Williams, 2020 ). Participatory and interactive planning brings residents, designers, planners and public-sector actors together over time. Here co-production means jointly making maps, prototypes and stewardship charters. Social innovation (Moulaert et al., 2013 ) combines the satisfaction of unmet needs, changed social relations and empowerment. It lets me ask how new relations, practices and rules emerge, how they shift who holds agency, and whether they are institutionalised beyond the project. Stewardship is analysed through Ostrom's (1990 ) design principles and research on urban green commons (Colding & Barthel, 2013 ; Colding et al., 2013 ), which defines these commons by the right to manage green space collectively rather than by who owns the land. GSIs are understood as circular infrastructures that close loops of biomass, water, tools and knowledge.

The literature documents projectification and stresses the value of participation. It says less about how agency across human and non-human actors changes over time, or how informal care arrangements get embedded in statutory planning (Power & Williams, 2020 ; Williams & Rodela, 2025 ). I address this with three elements: longitudinal relational actor mapping, co-designed stewardship charters, and a four-pillar agency matrix (decision rungs, deliberative authority, temporal agency and spatial control rights). Together they form a Resilient Urban Commons Framework that WeGrowCircle can use as a shared conceptual and methodological base.

4. Research design and methods

The study is a single-case study in the Aalborg Living Lab. Aalborg Øst and a local housing association are candidate site and partner, to be confirmed with the WeGrowCircle team. The methods are qualitative, participatory and oriented towards planning practice. I do not assume baseline deprivation but ask what agency diverse actors hold over green commons in an already regenerated district. Mapping serves two purposes: it is an analytical diagnostic and a generative tool for working with participants.

6. Feasibility, risks and ethics

The scope is deliberately limited: one site, one core method for each objective, and simple indicators. Access depends on the housing association and the municipality. If it is restricted, the design falls back to fewer interviews and workshops, which would still address SQ1 and SQ2.

I do not currently speak Danish. Interviews with institutional actors will be in English where possible. Fieldwork with residents relies on community interpreters and co-facilitation with Danish-speaking colleagues. Danish planning documents are read with translation tools.

The main risks and responses are participation fatigue (tangible early outcomes, and flexible scheduling because the time people can give varies widely; Colding & Barthel, 2013 ), power asymmetries and exclusion (subgroup sessions, non-text visual kits; Colding et al., 2013 ; Torrens & von Wirth, 2021 ), institutional capture (early involvement of maintenance staff), dependence on project funding (testing from year one how charters could connect to municipal maintenance, without assuming they can; Torrens & von Wirth, 2021 ), mismatch with planning cycles (outputs timed to policy windows identified in Phase 1) and the gap in ecological expertise (sought through the consortium). The research follows informed consent, the GDPR and AAU ethics review. I will keep a reflexive journal on my positionality as a planner.

Methodological Foundation

Why relational actor mapping?

Actor relations are mapped through interviews and participatory network drawing with residents and stakeholders, then read descriptively: who connects whom, which actors bridge groups, where gaps and frictions are. No advanced network statistics are required. The map is repeated at baseline (Phase 1) and re-mapping (Phase 3) to trace change qualitatively.

How the Pathway Model Works

This is an interactive conceptual simulation (not empirical field data) designed to illustrate the projectification critique in under 10 seconds:

  • Grant-Led Curve: Initial funding creates high care capacity. In this illustrative model, capacity declines after grant expiry (assumed pattern, not data).
  • Commons Curve: Starts with relationship building. Its trajectory and sustained capacity are dynamically shaped by the 3 governance switches: Decision Rights, Stewardship Charter, and Planning Anchoring (illustrative weights for conceptual demonstration, not estimated from data).
