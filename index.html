Analytical Core

Agency as the Central Analytical Concept

"Agency is defined as the capacity of actors to shape decisions, practices, and spaces."

Urban social resilience is not a fixed attribute of a neighborhood, but an emergent property generated when diverse actors gain the capacity to negotiate statutory rules and steward their physical environments. Agency is relational: it expands when institutions provide legal openings and physical spaces offer affordances for collective care.

Theoretical Pillars: Davoudi et al. (2012) Evolutionary Resilience • Healey (2007) Relational Planning • Ostrom (1990) Commoning • Brandsen et al. (2018) Co-Creation.

PhD Research Proposal • Aalborg University • WeGrowCircle

Emergent Urban Social Resilience: Mapping Agency in Actor Landscapes and Co-Designing Regenerative Green-Social Infrastructures as Urban Commons

Preliminary research proposal for the PhD position on Urban Social Resilience in the WeGrowCircle project.

Abstract

This project studies how urban social resilience emerges from the interplay of social dynamics, institutional frameworks, and material-spatial configurations. Taking a socio-material planning and design perspective, it asks how social actors, relations, and practices gain agency in resilience-building.

Through analytical and generative mapping and an inclusive co-design process, the project develops regenerative green-social infrastructures (GSIs) as urban commons in the Aalborg Living Lab, in close relation to local planning practice. It contributes a Resilient Urban Commons Framework, a tested co-creation approach, and recommendations for integrating GSIs into local planning, climate action, and community practice.

1. Background and Problem

Cities face climate impacts, social fragmentation, and biodiversity loss, and urban social resilience has become a pressing planning concern. Yet resilience is still often treated as a fixed outcome of policy or a pre-existing trait of communities. This view separates the social from the institutional and the material, and it overlooks who is able to act in resilience-building and who is not.

This project understands resilience differently: as an emergent quality produced through continuous interactions between actors and their relations, formal and informal institutions, and the spatial and ecological settings in which they live (Davoudi et al., 2012). If resilience is emergent, it cannot be delivered on demand. Planning and design can, however, cultivate the conditions from which it emerges: relations, capacities, institutional openings, and material settings.

Green-social infrastructures (GSIs) are a productive arena for studying this. They combine green spaces with the social relations and practices that sustain them, and they bring together residents, planners, designers, and public organisations. In practice, however, many participatory greening initiatives remain pilots. They give some actors limited agency, and they struggle to connect with statutory planning and long-term land management.

The research gap lies at the intersection of three areas that are usually studied separately: social resilience, socio-material understandings of planning, and the commoning and governance of green space. This project connects them.

2. Conceptual Framework

The project develops a Resilient Urban Commons Framework as a shared conceptual and methodological foundation for WeGrowCircle. It places urban social resilience at the centre and reads it through three interacting dimensions:

  • Social dynamics: actors, relations, practices, and agency, including trust, inclusion, and collective learning.
  • Institutional frameworks: regulations, plans, climate policies, land ownership, and governance arrangements across actors, organisations, and institutions.
  • Material-spatial configurations: land, green infrastructure, species, materials, and spatial layout, treated as active participants in producing resilience rather than a passive backdrop.

Four bodies of work support the framework:

  1. Resilience is approached critically and relationally, beyond equilibrium and recovery thinking (Davoudi et al., 2012).
  2. Socio-material planning attends to how people, institutions, and material settings co-produce places (Healey, 2007).
  3. Commons and commoning offer a lens on collective stewardship and rules for shared resources (Ostrom, 1990).
  4. Co-production and co-design frame participation as shared creation rather than consultation (Brandsen et al., 2018; Arnstein, 1969).

Within this framework, agency is defined as the capacity of actors to shape decisions, practices, and spaces, and it is the central analytical concept.

3. Aim, Research Questions, and Objectives

Aim: To understand and strengthen how urban social resilience emerges from the interplay of social, institutional, and material-spatial dynamics. The study maps how actors gain agency across actor landscapes and co-designs regenerative GSIs as urban commons, in close relation to local planning practice.

Main Research Question:

"How do social actors, relations, and practices gain agency in producing urban social resilience through the interplay of social, institutional, and material-spatial dimensions, and how can mapping, co-design, and planning processes strengthen this in regenerative GSIs?"

Sub-Research Questions:

  • SRQ1 (mapping): What actor landscapes, relations, practices, and spatial conditions shape resilience around GSIs in the Aalborg Living Lab, and where are the gaps in agency and inclusion?
  • SRQ2 (co-design): How can an inclusive co-design process build agency and the capacities for resilience among diverse actors?
  • SRQ3 (stewardship and planning): Which stewardship and governance arrangements sustain resilience, and how can they be embedded in local planning and climate action?

4. Research Design and Methodology

The project follows an action research and research-by-design approach in the Aalborg Living Lab, working alongside residents, planners, designers, and public actors. Because resilience is treated as emergent, the design is longitudinal and process-oriented, and it uses mapping twice, as a baseline and as a re-mapping, to trace change.

The 4 phases span:

  • Phase 1 (Year 1): Actor & relational mapping, participatory GIS, policy review, and baseline semi-structured interviews.
  • Phase 2 (Years 1.5–2): Workshops, design charrettes, and prototyping with generative maps as shared working material (Star & Griesemer, 1989).
  • Phase 3 (Years 2–3): Observation, follow-up interviews, re-mapping, and co-defined indicators for biodiversity, circular practices, sufficiency, and well-being.
  • Phase 4 (Year 3): Synthesis, framework refinement, policy dialogue, and statutory planning recommendations.

5. Expected Contributions

  • Theoretical: A conceptualisation of urban social resilience as an emergent socio-material quality, and a Resilient Urban Commons Framework linking agency, institutions, and spatial configurations.
  • Methodological: Repeated relational and spatial mapping as both an analytical and generative method, and a tested inclusive co-design process for GSIs.
  • Practical: Evidence and actionable recommendations for integrating GSIs into local planning, climate action, and community practice, including stewardship models.
